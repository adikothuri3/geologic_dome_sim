# 2026-W32 (Aug 3–9)

Phase 1 — MuJoCo fluency. Planned Aug 3–9; landed **Aug 2**, a week early, but not on the

## Sun Aug 2 — Phase 1 done, on Windows instead of WSL2

Demo: `phase1_stand.mp4` — G1 posed through stand → crouch → stand → t-pose → arms-up →
stand on a numpy-generated heightfield, orbiting camera, 471 frames / 18.8 s at 25 fps.
Contact sheet in `reports/phase1_contact_sheet.png`.

### What broke, in order

**1. `wsl --install` did nothing.** Looked like a hang. It wasn't — no `wsl`, `dism` or
installer process existed at all, `LxssManager` was never registered, nothing WSL-related
was staged for reboot. Root cause: `VirtualizationFirmwareEnabled = False`. **AMD SVM is
disabled in the BIOS** and Gigabyte ships B550 boards that way. No OS-side fix exists on
consumer Gigabyte boards — they expose no WMI BIOS provider the way Dell/Lenovo/HP business
machines do. Needs a physical trip to firmware: `shutdown /r /fw /t 0` → Tweaker → Advanced
CPU Settings → SVM Mode → Enabled.

Lesson: "stuck" is worth five minutes of process-list checking before waiting on it. Nothing
was running.

**2. Pivoted to native Windows.** Phase 1 turned out to need none of WSL: MuJoCo physics is
CPU, and Windows rendered through WGL with `MUJOCO_GL` unset on the first try — no EGL/OSMesa
fight at all, which was the risk I'd been most worried about. The whole environment was up in
about ten minutes.

**3. `git clone` failed TLS.** `unable to get local issuer certificate`. Already documented in
`notes/setup.md` from the skills install — global git config pins OpenSSL and fails against
GitHub on this box. `GIT_CONFIG_*` + `http.sslBackend=schannel` fixed it. The vault paid for
itself here; I'd have burned 20 minutes rediscovering that.

**4. `mediapy` needs a system ffmpeg.** Fine on Linux, absent on Windows. Swapped to
`imageio` + `imageio-ffmpeg`, which bundles its own binary — one code path for both OSes now.

**5. Duplicate keyframe name.** Declared my own `stand` key in the scene while `g1.xml`
already had one → `repeated name 'stand' in key`. Mine was a verbatim copy anyway, so I
deleted it and inherited Menagerie's. One fact, one home, even in XML.

**6. Meshes not found (the fiddly one).** `<include>`ing `g1.xml` from `sim/` broke every
mesh path. `g1.xml` declares `meshdir="assets"`, and MuJoCo resolves that relative to the
**top-level** scene file, not to the included file — Menagerie's own `scene.xml` never hits
this because it sits beside `g1.xml`. Two false starts before I stopped theorising and wrote
a probe script trying five configurations. Answer: our `<compiler>` must come **after** the
`<include>` (MJCF applies elements in order, so putting it first lets `g1.xml`'s own meshdir
win), with `meshdir` relative to `sim/`.

Also learned the error message names a *random* missing mesh each run — MuJoCo loads meshes
in parallel. I briefly read that as "progress" between attempts. It wasn't.

**7. Sphere drop test failed, and the test was wrong.** Three of four probes reported "still
moving." The spheres were **rolling downhill** — MuJoCo models sliding friction, not rolling
resistance, so a sphere never settles on a 4° slope. The one that passed was the flat spawn
pad, which should have been the tell. Switched the probe to a box. The `mjcf-terrain` skill
says "sphere/box"; for sloped terrain it has to be a box.

Worth noting the heights matched `sample_height` to within a centimetre even while failing —
that's the row/col orientation empirically confirmed, which is what Phase 4 actually needs.

**8. The robot fell — on the last transition only.** Held stand, crouch, stand, t-pose,
arms-up all at 0.0 cm drift, then toppled going arms-up → stand. Swinging both arms down
1.8 rad in one second dumps angular momentum into a floating base with straight legs and no
ankle strategy to absorb it. Not a terrain problem. Fixed with a slew-rate limit on the
position targets (`MAX_JOINT_RATE = 0.8 rad/s`, so that blend stretches to 2.25 s) plus
smoothstep easing so the rate is zero at both ends.

This is the most useful thing I learned all day: **position actuators hold joint angles, not
balance.** Phase 1 gets away with posing only because the spawn pad is level. Everything a
policy is for starts exactly here.

**9. Terrain looked flat on camera.** Demo passed all assertions, but 6 cm of relief over a
6 m field is invisible at a downward camera angle — and my own verification criterion said
"visibly non-flat terrain." Raised `elevation_z` to 0.15 m and dropped the camera to −8°
so relief reads in silhouette against the sky. Passing assertions ≠ a good demo.

### Experiment: `FLAT_PAD_FRAC = 0` — what the pad is actually holding up

Ran the demo with the level spawn pad disabled, to see the boundary rather than assume it.
Evidence: `reports/phase1_fall.png` (the topple), `reports/phase1_fall_poses.png` (after).

It fell in **under a second**, during the 1 s settle, before the pose sequence even started:

```
settled: pelvis 0.383 m above terrain        # with the pad: 0.792 m
stand  held 2.0s  clearance 0.081 m  drift 26.2 cm
```

Spawned on ~10° of slope, straight legs locked, CoM outside the support polygon. No recovery
mechanism exists, so there was no recovery.

**The result worth keeping** is what the log said *after* it fell:

```
crouch   held 1.5s  clearance 0.101 m  drift 0.0 cm
t_pose   held 1.5s  clearance 0.073 m  drift 0.0 cm
arms_up  held 1.5s  clearance 0.085 m  drift 0.0 cm
```

**0.0 cm drift — perfect joint tracking — while flat on its back.** All 29 actuators held
their commanded angles to a fraction of a degree the whole time. `kp=500` knows exactly one
thing: joint error. It does not know about gravity, base orientation, or contact. The robot
performed a crisp t-pose in the dirt and reported success.

Two consequences:

- **The `never collapsed` assertion is load-bearing, not decoration.** Judging this run by
  tracking error alone would have scored it a pass. Any Phase 5 eval harness needs a base
  height/orientation term that is *independent* of joint tracking, or a fallen policy looks
  good on the metrics that are easiest to log.
- **~10° of slope is not mild** for a straight-legged stand — I'd assumed it was. The pad is a
  bigger crutch than the slope statistics suggested.

This is now the baseline for Phase 2: a policy standing unaided with `FLAT_PAD_FRAC = 0` is a
real before/after, and the failing video exists to compare against.

## Mon Aug 3 — WSL2 environment up; five install attempts to get there

SVM enabled in firmware, reboot done, and the whole Linux toolchain is now live: Ubuntu 24.04,
hardware EGL, jax 0.11.0 on `CudaDevice(id=0)`, Playground 0.2.0, `G1JoystickFlatTerrain`
loading and stepping. **Phase 1 reproduces identically on Linux** — same drop test, same
assertions, same MP4 — which is the parity check worth having before Phase 2 starts.

It took five runs of `setup_wsl.sh`. Worth writing down, because only one of the five failures
was environmental.

### The install failures, in order

**1. PowerShell ate a `$` meant for bash.** `setup_wsl_stage2.ps1` built the user-creation
script in a double-quoted here-string containing `\$(id $UnixUser)` — backslash escapes in
bash, but PowerShell escapes with a backtick, so it evaluated `$(id aditya)` itself and tried
to run `id` as a cmdlet. With `$ErrorActionPreference = 'Stop'` the script died before the
user existed. Fixed with a single-quoted here-string (`@'...'@`, no interpolation at all) and
an explicit `__USER__` placeholder.

Related, and worse: PowerShell also mangles multi-word output coming back from `wsl.exe`
(`user ok: uid=1000(aditya)` arrived as just `user`). At one point that made a *failed* run
report `EXIT=0`. A false success is more expensive than a failure — everything now runs as a
script invoked by path, with the exit code captured inside bash where PowerShell can't reach it.

**2. `curl: (28) SSL connection timeout` on the uv installer.** Looked transient.

**3. Same timeout, different host** — `git clone` of Menagerie. Two hosts, two runs, so not
transient. Measured it: **~1 in 5 fresh HTTPS connections from WSL hangs until timeout, while
a sustained transfer runs fine at 6.3 MB/s** (507 MB pulled from codeload as proof). It's
connection setup that's flaky, not bandwidth.

Also caught myself here: my `retry()` wrapper hadn't fired, and my first instinct was that
retry was broken. It wasn't — the clone *succeeded*, and the failure was in the unwrapped
`git sparse-checkout set` on the next line, which triggers a lazy blob fetch on a
`--filter=blob:none` clone. That's a fresh connection, and it drew the unlucky one. **The
command that fails is the one that needs wrapping, not the one nearby that looks like it.**

Real fix was to delete the network call instead of retrying it: the Windows box already has a
sparse Menagerie clone that Phase 1 renders from, so the Linux side now copies `unitree_g1`
out of it. Zero network on the common path.

**4. `uv venv` refuses an existing venv.** Run 2 created it before dying at Menagerie, so run 3
tripped over it immediately. Every failure leaves partial state behind, so *every* step has to
be re-runnable — now guarded on `[[ -x "$VENV/bin/python" ]]`.

**5. The one-hour "hang" that wasn't mine.** `check_phase2.py` sat for over an hour, then died
with no log. Two things stacked: `registry.load()` clones Playground's **own** pinned Menagerie
(~500 MB, commit `1b86ece`) on first use, and that clone drew a stalled connection; meanwhile
WSL auto-terminated the idle distro, which killed the process and wiped `/tmp` — where I was
writing the log. `systemd=true` in `wsl.conf` mounts `/tmp` as tmpfs. Logs now go to `/mnt/c/`.

### The finding that actually matters for Phase 2

Chasing that hang surfaced something better than the fix:

```
Failed to allocate device memory of 6.00GiB: CUDA_ERROR_OUT_OF_MEMORY
  ... retried 5.40GiB, 4.86GiB, 4.37GiB
```

JAX preallocates 75 % of VRAM — 0.75 × 8188 MiB is *exactly* the 6.00 GiB that failed. It
fails because this GPU also drives the Windows desktop, which is holding ~2 GB. XLA backs off
until something fits, so nothing crashes; it just starts every run with a fragmented pool.

**The real training budget is ~6 GB, not 8.** Both scripts now set
`XLA_PYTHON_CLIENT_PREALLOCATE=false`. This is the kind of thing that would have looked like
"MJX is mysteriously slow" three weeks from now.

### DimOS: isolated on purpose, and it paid off immediately

`dimos[base,unitree]` resolves ~289 packages including torch and a second full CUDA stack,
plus its own numpy pin — into the same venv as MJX, as originally written. That trades a
working Phase 2 (due Aug 16) against a Phase 6 integration (due Sept 27). Moved it to
`~/venvs/dimos`.

First run in isolation failed on `pyaudio` needing `Python.h` and portaudio headers — and the
Phase 2 environment was untouched, exactly as intended. Added `python3.12-dev` +
`portaudio19-dev`.

## Tue Aug 4 — first two locomotion policies, and full-body collision

Two 2048-env runs landed. Videos: `reports/baseline_feetonly.mp4` and
`reports/full_collision_200M.mp4` (both walking forward at 1 m/s, 8–10 s, neither falls).

| Run | Collision | Steps | Peak | Final | Wall |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `2026-08-04-g1-joystick` | feet-only | 100M | **4.53** @ 78M | 2.96 | 27 min |
| `2026-08-04-g1-joystick-full-collision` | full-body | 200M | **6.18** @ 84M | 1.58 | 59 min |

### The finding that matters: both peak near 80M, then decline

Not a plateau — a drift downward, over many consecutive evals. The feet-only run ended 1.6
below its own peak, the full-collision run **4.6** below.

And brax returns the *final* params. So both saved policies are worse than the best policy
the run actually found, and training longer made the saved artefact worse while the log looked
like it was merely flattening out. Doubling to 200M did not buy a better policy; it bought
more room to decay.

Fixed by checkpointing every evaluation via brax's `policy_params_fn`, plus a `best.json`
recording which step won. `render_policy.py --best` renders that one. Checkpoints are a few MB
and there are only `num_evals` of them — a trivial price for not discarding the best result of
an hour's compute.

Eval std is ±8–10 throughout, so any single eval is noisy; the *trend* is what's persuasive,
not any one number. Worth remembering before reading much into a single reward value.

### Full-body collision cost far less than expected

Collidable geoms went 1 → 24 (torso, pelvis, hips, thighs, shins, shoulders, arms, hands,
feet), each sized from the Menagerie mesh of the body it wraps.

Throughput cost: ~5–8 % (21M steps in 7.5 min vs 22.3M in 7.3 min). Learning curves are nearly
identical early on — at ~11M steps, −2.31 vs −2.38 — and full-collision reached a *higher*
peak. So the honest summary is that it costs almost nothing and, on this evidence, does not
make flat-ground walking harder.

What it actually buys is not reward, it is **correctness**: with feet-only collision the legs
can pass through each other and through the torso, so a policy is free to learn a gait that
cannot exist on the real robot. That is the worst class of sim2real gap — invisible in sim,
fatal on hardware. On Everest scree, shin and knee contact is normal rather than an error
state, which is the real reason to carry it.

### Two upstream faults the gate caught

`check_full_collision.py` refused to pass the first two attempts, both times correctly:

1. **`left_thigh` and `left_shin` interpenetrate by 16 mm at the nominal pose.** Harmless while
   nothing collides; the moment broadphase was on it became a separating force that toppled the
   robot before it took a step (`pelvis_z` 0.79 → 0.227 during the settle). Overlapping body
   pairs are now detected and excluded rather than guessed at.
2. **The shin capsules are radius 0.08** — a 16 cm-thick shin — reaching low enough to graze
   the floor while merely *standing*. That would have fed permanent phantom ground contact into
   `feet_slip`, `feet_air_time` and the contact sensors, corrupting the reward for reasons no
   log would have explained. Refitted to the mesh (9 cm), which also cut required exclusions
   from 6 to 2 and so *preserved* more genuine self-contact.

Both were invisible in a render and would have been near-impossible to diagnose from a reward
curve. This is the argument for asserting physical properties instead of eyeballing a video.

Side effect: `left_foot_right_shin_found` / `right_foot_left_shin_found` now work. Upstream's
`_get_termination()` reads both to catch leg crossing, but their `<pair>` entries are commented
out, so that half of the termination test could never fire in the feet-only baseline.

### Process notes

- **A run must not depend on the process that launched it.** The first 200M attempt was killed
  at 67M steps because the harness stopped the background task, and killing `wsl.exe` takes the
  whole WSL session with it. Relaunched under `setsid`; it then survived to completion.
- **A SIGKILLed run writes no `experiments.md` row** — Python's `finally` never runs. The row
  is the record, so the run has to survive to write it.
- **`/tmp` is tmpfs under `systemd=true`.** An idle-terminated distro took a log with it, and
  the evidence for a failure vanished with the failure. Logs now go to `/mnt/c/`.
- My progress monitor reported `PROCESS GONE without EXIT=` on a run that had actually
  succeeded — it checks for the exit marker, then for a live process, and the run exited in the
  window between. False alarm, but the ordering is worth fixing before it cries wolf again.
  **Update:** adding a 10 s grace period did not fix it — it misfired again on the 100M run,
  which had also succeeded. The log lives on the `/mnt/c` drvfs mount and the final write lands
  slower than that. Two false deaths, zero true ones: the monitor's death signal is not
  trustworthy and the log is the authority.

## Tue Aug 4 (later) — Phase 2 closed out: best-checkpoint policy + the stretch experiment

### The corrected premise

Spent part of this session about to "add" domain randomization and parallel envs. **Both were
already running in every policy trained so far.** `config.json` records `num_envs: 2048` and
`train_g1.py` passes `randomization_fn=registry.get_domain_randomizer(...)`, which is
`@jax.vmap`'d with `in_axes` mapping each randomized field to axis 0 — so all 2048 envs train
on *different physics simultaneously*, not one shared domain.

Worth the check before building: the roadmap's Phase 2 asks to **understand** what domain
randomization is doing, not to add it, and explicitly places terrain randomization, motor
strength and sensor latency in **Phase 5**. Written up in [[locomotion-policy]].

### The 100M run, with checkpointing

`best 4.74 @ 95M`, `final 2.65` — and this time the best was kept. It is genuinely better in
rollout, not just on the noisy eval metric: +0.0285 vs +0.0256 reward/step over a fixed-command
10 s clip, ~11 %.

It did **not** reproduce the 200M run's 6.18. brax splits its RNG per eval epoch, so a
100M/20-eval run is not the first half of a 200M/20-eval one — it is a different trajectory
that happened to land at 4.74. Both sit in the same 4.5–6.2 band. The 6.18 policy itself is
gone for good; it predates checkpointing.

### Stretch experiment: `feet_air_time` 2.0 → 4.0

Everything else held constant — same collision model, 2048 envs, 100M steps, seed 0.

**Total reward is not the comparison.** Changing a reward *weight* changes the scale the run is
scored on, so 3.05 vs 4.74 says nothing about which gait is better. `scripts/compare_gaits.py`
measures behaviour instead, under an identical command:

| Metric | baseline | `feet_air_time` ×2 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| swing fraction (share of time a foot is airborne) | 0.35 / 0.39 | **0.40 / 0.40** |
| steps per second per foot | 1.50 / 1.60 | 1.60 / 1.80 |
| forward velocity tracked (command 1.0 m/s) | **0.833** | 0.817 |
| velocity error | **0.167** | 0.183 |
| pelvis height | 0.750 ± 0.014 | 0.753 ± 0.016 |

The result is exactly what the term is meant to do, and it cost what you would expect:
**paying double for air time bought more air time** — swing fraction up, step rate up, gait
symmetrised (0.35/0.39 → 0.40/0.40) — **at the price of the thing you actually wanted**, with
velocity tracking degrading from 0.167 to 0.183 error. Reward terms compete; buying more of one
is paid for out of the others.

A separate observation from the same table: *both* policies track ~0.83 m/s against a 1.0 m/s
command, a consistent ~17 % undershoot. `tracking_lin_vel` is `exp(−err²/0.25)`, which is
generously flat near the optimum, so there is little gradient pressure to close that last bit.
Worth remembering before reading a Phase 5 velocity number as ground truth.

Videos: `reports/phase2_best_walk.mp4`, `phase2_best_turn.mp4` (same policy turning in place),
`phase2_stretch_airtime4.mp4`, `phase2_final_walk.mp4`.

## Tue Aug 4 (night) — Phase 3 toolchain up; LingBot-Map on an indoor clip

LingBot-Map installed from zero (`notes/setup.md` had no row for it), and `recon/` now goes
video → point cloud + camera trajectory → Open3D and viser. Ran it on `room_map.MOV`, a 2:12
handheld walkthrough of a bedroom.

**This does not close Phase 3.** The roadmap's demo is a *local outdoor trail* — rocky trail,
boulder field, steep stairs, rubble. A room is not that. But it was the right first clip
anyway: it doubled as the white-wall snow proxy, and it found a limit that would otherwise
have surfaced on Everest footage in October.

### The clip fought back before the model even saw it

Two properties of modern iPhone video that would have quietly degraded everything:

1. **Dolby Vision / HLG 10-bit HDR** (`transfer=arib-std-b67`, `bt2020nc`). Decoded naively
   to 8-bit sRGB the frames come out washed out and low-contrast — and contrast is precisely
   the signal a feed-forward depth model keys on. Needs a real tonemap
   (`zscale` → linear → `tonemap=hable` → BT.709), not a pixel-format cast.
2. **Portrait stored as landscape + a `displaymatrix: rotation of -90` side-data tag.**

Neither is visible if you just look at the video. `extract_frames.py` handles both and
*asserts* the resulting orientation rather than trusting ffmpeg's autorotation.

### Aspect ratio was worth 1.8 GB of VRAM

LingBot-Map fits **width** to 518 and only crops height. So:

- portrait 9:16 → `pad` mode → 518×518, of which **43% is white padding**
- the same frame rotated to landscape → `crop` mode → **518×294**, no padding, no cropping

Same field of view, 43% fewer tokens. Measured on an identical 60-frame clip: peak VRAM
**6.93 → 5.15 GB**, 3.17 → 4.07 fps, and *more* surviving points (409k → 451k). Rotating the
footage is strictly better than padding it. Added `--rotate-cw`.

(`pad` still matters for portrait input you can't rotate: upstream's hardcoded `mode="crop"`
would centre-crop 921 px of height down to 518, throwing away the near-field floor — the exact
thing Phase 4 turns into terrain. When padding is used, `reconstruct.py` detects the letterbox
border and masks it, or the depth head's predictions on white padding spray a fake plane into
the cloud.)

### Three ways this box says "no", none of them a normal OOM

1. **`OSError: [Errno 12] Cannot allocate memory`** loading ~1300 JPEGs — nothing to do with
   memory, it's drvfs. Frames have to be staged on ext4, not `/mnt/c`.
2. **OOM-killed at 7 GB RSS.** Predictions accumulate on the CPU in fp32 — images + depth +
   depth_conf ≈ 7.1 GB for 1320 frames, against 7.7 GB of WSL RAM. Worse, the run **reported
   exit code 0**, because `python … | grep …` returns grep's status. A false success is the
   expensive kind of failure; that's twice this week (the PowerShell one on Aug 3).
   `reconstruct.py` now preflights the RAM estimate and aborts in 5 s instead of dying 20 min in.
3. **`CUDA driver error: device not ready`, and once `Wsl/Service/E_UNEXPECTED`** — overshooting
   VRAM under WSL doesn't reliably raise a clean torch OOM; it can take the whole distro down
   (`wsl --shutdown` to recover). Setting `torch.cuda.set_per_process_memory_fraction` converts
   that into a catchable `torch.OutOfMemoryError`, which is how the `window_size 32` attempt
   failed afterwards — loudly and harmlessly.

Also: the 4.63 GB checkpoint doesn't fit the obvious load path. Upstream's
`torch.load(map_location=device)` puts it on the GPU *and then* allocates the model again on
`.to(device)`; loading with `mmap=True` and casting to bf16 before the transfer lands weights
at 2.81 GB.

### The finding that matters: ~25 seconds of footage per consistent scene

> [!warning] Corrected the next day — read the Aug 5 entry
> The version of this section written on the night of Aug 4 concluded "streaming mode is out,
> so windowed is mandatory, so stitching is the problem." The first clause was **my bug**, and
> it made the whole chain of reasoning wrong in an interesting way. Kept below as written,
> because the stitching evidence is real and the correction is the more useful lesson.

Streaming mode looked out — 660 frames pinned the card at 7858/8188 MiB and simply stopped
progressing (33/660 after ten minutes). So windowed mode seemed mandatory, which means windows
must be stitched.

They weren't. `inference_windowed` warps each window into the first window's frame using a
similarity transform estimated from overlapping keyframes, and on plain cream walls that
estimate is wrong:

| Windows | overlap keyframes | traj length | scene extent | ratio |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13 | 4 | 88.6 | 2.60 | **34×** |
| 9 | 12 | 109.6 | 2.88 | **38×** |
| **1** | — | 6.75 | 2.45 | **2.8×** |

A camera path 34× the size of the room it walks around is not a camera path. There's a 2.14
pose jump at frame 164 against a median step of 0.071, right at a window boundary, and the
rendered trajectory is a scribble.

The instinct — "increase the overlap" — made it *worse*, which is the useful part. The failure
is **scale estimation on low-texture overlap**, not insufficient overlap. And that ties this
directly to the snow question: snow is the same texture regime, so this is not an indoor
curiosity. Per-frame geometry was fine throughout; what low texture cost us was *global
consistency*, which is a much easier failure to miss.

Deliverable is therefore the single-window run: 124 frames (25 s), 921k points, coherent room,
smooth trajectory, no pose jump. `run.json` now records `traj_length_over_extent` so this is
caught automatically next time — ≲3 healthy, tens means the stitch failed.

## Wed Aug 5 — the streaming conclusion was wrong, and the real limit is ~25 seconds

Aditya asked the obvious question I should have asked myself: *doesn't Pemba run in streaming
mode?* The entire project premise is streaming reconstruction from a live camera, and I had just
written "streaming is not usable past ~100 frames" into five files. Those two things cannot both
be true.

They weren't. `recon/reconstruct.py` had:

```python
if a.mode == "streaming":
    images = images.to(device)          # ← all 660 frames, 1.2 GB, onto an 8 GB card
else:
    print("keeping inputs on CPU; windows move to GPU one at a time")
```

with a comment asserting streaming "has no such slicing, so it still needs the tensor resident."
`gct_stream.py` says the opposite in as many words: *"we slice-then-move per iteration so peak
GPU memory is O(scale) or O(1) frames rather than O(S)."* I had read that exact sentence in the
windowed model and generalised the wrong way. One line deleted; streaming then ran the entire
660-frame clip at a **flat 5.63 GB**.

**Lesson, and it is the same one as `retry()` on Aug 3:** when a measurement contradicts the
premise the whole project rests on, suspect the measurement. I had the contradiction in hand —
"streaming model can't stream" — and wrote it into the vault as a finding instead of a symptom.

### What the limit actually is

With streaming working, I could measure the thing that matters. Same footage, both modes,
`traj_length_over_extent` (camera path length ÷ scene size; ≲3 healthy):

| Clip length | streaming | windowed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 25 s | **2.76** | **2.76** — byte-identical, 921,100 points |
| 49 s | 11.8 | 9.7 |
| 132 s | 34–46 | 31–38 |

They agree *exactly* at 25 s and fail together past it. So stitching was never the root cause —
it was one of two symptoms. The KV cache that keeps streaming memory bounded is also the model's
memory horizon (~24 keyframes ≈ 14 s here), and once the scene falls out of it, global
consistency goes: as scale jumps at window boundaries (windowed, per-window scale span measured
at **3.2×**) or as smooth drift (streaming).

Two other dead ends worth recording, both closed cleanly:

- **Sample the clip sparser to fit one window.** No: ≥5 fps is required. 0.2 s between frames
  gives 2.8, 0.3 s gives 8.6. The cliff is sharp, and it means you cannot trade frame rate for
  duration.
- **Drop the resolution to afford a bigger window.** No: `size mismatch for
  aggregator.patch_embed.pos_embed: [1,1370,1024] vs [1,577,1024]`. 518 is baked into the
  checkpoint.

### The consequence for the expedition

This upgrades the cloud-GPU question from throughput to **correctness**. A bigger card buys a
bigger KV cache, which buys a longer memory horizon, which is the only lever that extends the
25 s window. Two things to test before September: `lingbot-map-long` (built for this regime,
still not downloaded), and feeding Pemba's odometry as an external pose prior — the one signal
the expedition has that a handheld phone clip does not.

And the capture rule that follows is worth more than any of the tuning: **shoot for coverage,
not duration.** A deliberate 20–25 s sweep of a space reconstructs cleanly; two minutes of
wandering the same space does not, and no amount of subsampling rescues it.

## Aug 6 — courthouse abandoned, the whole chain closes on `loop`

Two goals today: get an *outdoor* reconstruction, and finish Phase 4 on something.

### Why upstream's courthouse demo doesn't reproduce here

Read `demo.py`'s defaults instead of guessing: `kv_cache_sliding_window=64`, and
`keyframe_interval` auto-selected to 1 for anything under the 320-view RoPE limit. That is
the config behind every published demo. Our courthouse runs used 16 and 24, because 64 OOMs.
So the published result isn't a different scene or a secret flag — it's the same config with
4× the memory horizon.

I spent six runs trying to buy that horizon back and failed at every angle: kvsw 64 and 48
both OOM even after halving the sequence with `--stride 2`, `lingbot-map-long` OOMs at 286
frames outright, and windowed mode — upstream's own documented remedy for pose collapse —
OOMs too. **kvsw ≈ 24 is a hard ceiling here and it is set by cache size, not sequence
length.** That last part surprised me; I'd assumed a shorter clip would free room for a
bigger cache. It doesn't.

The failure mode is worth describing precisely, because it's not what "bad reconstruction"
usually looks like. Per-frame geometry on courthouse is *excellent* — you can read the
banners on the building and count window mullions. What collapses is global pose: a 112-unit
camera path inside a 4.5-unit scene, `traj_length_over_extent` **24.9** against loop's 3.36,
and the facade stacked in visibly offset layers. Good depth, garbage poses. And it's a cliff,
not a gradient: doubling the horizon via `keyframe_interval 2` moved 24.88 → 25.10. Nothing.

Also built `--mask_sky` into `reconstruct.py` today. Upstream only wires sky segmentation
into its *viewer*, so our exported `.ply` never got it — on an overcast outdoor scene that
matters, since blown-out sky is a large, smooth, *confidently wrong* surface. Running skyseg
on the preprocessed frames rather than the originals sidesteps every crop/resize alignment
question. It removed 121k points. Kept, because the Everest footage will need it.

### Phase 4, and four false alarms

Pivoted to `loop` and took it all the way: scale → cleanup → hfield → drop test → G1. The
gates caught four bugs, and every one of them initially looked like a terrain defect:

1. **The ground plane was a wall.** Picking the RANSAC plane with the most inliers is wrong
   in a corridor — the walls carry more points than the carpet. Symptom: camera heights
   spanning 0.33–6.22 units, when a walking camera obviously holds a near-constant height.
   Fix: choose the plane that *minimises* camera-height spread. That's what "floor" means.
2. **The drop probe fell off the map.** My flat-cell search picked cells at the exact field
   boundary, so the box had half its footprint over the void. Reported as "tunnelled".
3. **The robot stood on a desk.** The flattest observed patch in an office is a desk top.
   It settled there very happily and the gate passed, having tested nothing.
4. **"Penetration" was the robot's own knee.** The gate measured max penetration over *all*
   contacts, so the crouch pose's thigh-against-calf overlap read as 5 mm of terrain
   penetration. Filtering to terrain contacts and attributing them by body name gave the real
   number: 0.3 mm on flat ground, ~5 mm where a foot corner loads a sloped cell — which is
   Menagerie's foot compliance, not our geometry.

The one *real* finding underneath all that: **robust max-z is wrong indoors.** The skill
prescribes it, and it's right for an outdoor trail where the top surface is what you walk on.
In an office it turns every cubicle partition into a 2.3 m serrated spire and the heightfield
becomes a canyon — the render of the G1 wedged between two of them is the clearest picture of
a correct rule applied outside its domain that I've produced on this project. Added
`--surface ground` (near-minimum per cell, reject cells >0.4 m above the floor) and left
`top` as the default, because the trail footage this actually targets wants `top`.

Second real finding: **monocular depth noise is load-bearing.** The reconstructed carpet
carries ~5 cm of roughness — consistent with the 4.5 cm floor-band RMS measured off the raw
cloud — and that is enough to topple a keyframe-posed G1 outright. `--smooth 2` is what makes
it stand. That number goes in the asset JSON, because it changes the terrain a policy sees,
and Phase 5 has to know it's there.

Final state: 4.07M points → 964k cleaned and metric at 2.4938 m/unit → a 572×357 hfield at
5 cm with 0.39 m relief → drop test 6/6 clean (≤0.19 cm) → G1 settling at pelvis 0.741 m,
0.1 cm post-settle drift. Phase 1's synthetic terrain still passes, so nothing regressed.

Honest limits on the artifact: only **9% of the field is observed ground**, the rest is
filled flat; `ground` mode deliberately drops all furniture and walls, so this is a walking
surface with no obstacles; and the terrain is an office corridor, which is not remotely the
rocky trail the roadmap's Phase 4 demo calls for. What's proven is the *plumbing*, end to
end, with gates that have now demonstrated they catch things.
